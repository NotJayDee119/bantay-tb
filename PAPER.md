INTRODUCTION
Tuberculosis (TB) remains the world's leading cause of death from a single infectious disease, reclaiming that position in 2023 after being surpassed by COVID-19 during the pandemic years of 2020 to 2022. According to the World Health Organization (WHO) Global Tuberculosis Report 2024, an estimated 10.8 million people fell ill with TB worldwide in 2023, representing a 0.9 percent increase from 10.7 million cases in 2022, while TB-related deaths reached 1.25 million [1][2]. The disease disproportionately burdens low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), which account for 98 percent of global cases. Five countries—India (26%), Indonesia (10%), China (6.8%), the Philippines (6.8%), and Pakistan (6.3%)—together account for 56 percent of the global TB burden [3]. Despite decades of international control efforts, global TB incidence has declined by only 8.3 percent since 2015, far short of the WHO End TB Strategy's milestone of a 50 percent reduction by 2025, underscoring the persistent inadequacy of current surveillance and detection systems [4].
A critical dimension of TB's global persistence is the spatial heterogeneity of its distribution. Studies conducted in Brazil, India, and Peru have consistently demonstrated that TB cases are not randomly distributed within populations but cluster in geographically concentrated hotspots, often within specific urban barangays, wards, or districts where poverty, overcrowding, and limited healthcare access converge [5][6][7]. In Brazil, geospatial analysis of TB cases in high-incidence cities revealed strong spatial clustering correlated with low Human Development Index scores, identifying specific neighborhoods with case densities far exceeding city-level averages [6]. In Lima, Peru, geocoded routine TB case data enabled identification of localized transmission hotspots that aggregate district-level reports had consistently failed to detect, supporting earlier intervention response by health authorities [7].
In Mysuru district, India, GIS-based hotspot analysis of over 10,000 pulmonary TB cases from 2019 to 2023 identified persistent urban clusters affecting the elderly and individuals with diabetes, demonstrating that geospatially targeted interventions and continuous GIS-based surveillance are necessary for meaningful TB burden reduction [5]. Collectively, this international evidence establishes GIS-based spatial surveillance not as an optional enhancement but as a foundational component of effective TB control in high-burden settings. Additionally, only 44 percent of the estimated 400,000 individuals who developed multidrug-resistant TB (MDR-TB) in 2023 were diagnosed and treated, while global funding for TB prevention and care stood at only US$5.7 billion against a required annual target of US$22 billion [8]. These figures confirm that the global TB response requires not only increased investment but structural innovation in how surveillance data is collected, visualized, and acted upon at the community level.
The integration of Geographic Information System (GIS) technology into tuberculosis surveillance has moved well beyond the status of an experimental enhancement; it is now internationally recognized as an essential component of effective TB hotspot detection and control. A systematic review of TB spatial clustering studies published across MEDLINE- and ScienceDirect-indexed literature found strong evidence that TB is prone to spatial clustering in both developed and developing countries, and explicitly recommended GIS as the standard tool for TB surveillance and control programs worldwide. The review documented a significant and sustained rise in the use of GIS in TB epidemiology over the study period, particularly for identifying clusters and etiologic factors that non-spatial reporting systems are structurally incapable of detecting [9].
Furthermore, a review in BMC Public Health, a high-impact Springer Nature journal, analyzed 79 published studies spanning four decades of GIS-based TB surveillance research across China, Brazil, Iran, Ethiopia, and other high-burden countries, and explicitly identified GIS as one of the essential tools for TB surveillance, capable of mapping disease distribution and identifying high-incidence areas to target interventions and resources where they are most needed [10]. This evidence was further reinforced by a 2024 peer-reviewed review in the World Journal of Advanced Research and Reviews, which described GIS as a "powerful tool" in disease surveillance that "empowers public health professionals to make evidence-based decisions, allocate resources efficiently, and implement targeted interventions crucial for mitigating health risks," while noting that traditional surveillance systems face critical limitations in interpreting spatial disease patterns that GIS directly addresses [11].
In the Philippine context, researchers from De La Salle University–Dasmariñas and the University of the Philippines Manila used GIS visualization and Getis–Ord spatial analysis on TB case data from Region III for 2019, 2020, and 2021. The results confirmed significant geographic clustering of TB cases in 2019, identifying specific high-burden municipalities and cities that aggregate regional statistics had failed to distinguish. The study explicitly states that its results may be used for optimal resource allocation in addressing the spread of the disease and recommends GIS as an evidence-based framework for TB response in the Philippine setting. The non-significant results for 2020 and 2021 are attributed to COVID-19 underreporting rather than to the absence of geographic clustering [12].
GIS and remote sensing are already playing an important and growing role in Philippine public health. Key findings include: the Maternal GIS Project, which developed a health database for over 2,410 rural health units and 690 hospitals across the Philippines using GIS; the use of GIS in mapping biological threats, disease prevalence, and spatial-temporal patterns of infectious diseases; and GIS studies in Cabanatuan City, Nueva Ecija, which successfully mapped dengue hotspots at the barangay level, identifying demographic patterns invisible to non-spatial surveillance. The paper concludes that modeling local data through GIS can provide insights that lead to the development of programs better serving the community [13].
The Philippines remains one of the thirty countries with the highest burden of tuberculosis worldwide, carrying a significant share of TB cases in Southeast Asia [14]. As a result, health workers often have to manually review numerical case counts and compare reports to estimate which areas might be at higher risk. This process is time-consuming and may not support rapid responses during potential outbreaks. The Department of Health (DOH) has set a national target of achieving a 90 percent TB case detection rate; however, the Philippines continues to fall below this goal, with gaps in geographic surveillance identified as one of the contributing factors. In Baguio City, the TB treatment success rate is 81 percent, below the 90 percent goal [15]. According to the World Bank's collection of development indicators, assembled from officially recognized sources, the Philippines' tuberculosis case detection rate (all forms) was recorded at 78 percent in 2023 [16].
In Davao City, the consequences of this surveillance gap are particularly acute. The Davao City Health Office (CHO) documented 8,212 confirmed TB cases in 2023, rising to 8,601 in 2024, placing the city among the highest TB-burden urban centers in Mindanao [17]. The city's TB case detection rate remains below the DOH target of 90 percent, reflecting persistent gaps in diagnosis and reporting. These gaps are further exacerbated by urban overcrowding, poverty, and limited access to healthcare, which continue to drive transmission in high-burden barangays [16][18]. Barangay health workers and city TB coordinators continue to rely on spreadsheets, logbooks, and the aggregate ITIS dashboard to manage surveillance activities, none of which provide the geographic granularity required to detect clustering patterns, prioritize community outreach, or trigger early hotspot interventions in the city's most severely affected areas [19][20]. City data also show a rising trend in some districts (e.g., Talomo South and Buhangin). Talomo South recorded the most cases, with 505 in 2024 and 462 in 2023. Buhangin followed with 463 cases in 2024 and 453 in 2023. Bunawan recorded 459 cases in 2024, while Agdao recorded 435 cases in 2023. The majority of TB patients are working-class individuals between the ages of 18 and 40 [21].
Similar limitations are observed in tuberculosis information systems across Southeast Asia. The Philippines' ITIS, Indonesia's Sistem Informasi Tuberkulosis (SITB), and Vietnam's VITIMES platform support case recording and reporting but lack GIS-based hotspot detection, spatial clustering analysis, and community-level geographic visualization [22][23][24][25]. These systems primarily focus on administrative data management and do not provide the spatial intelligence necessary for real-time, localized public health response [22][23][24][25]. Alongside ITIS, the DOH, in partnership with the World Health Organization, also developed a set of companion tools called the End TB App Suite. This includes Care TB, which helps monitor whether patients are taking their medications regularly, and a separate monitoring dashboard called Race TB, which allows health program managers to track progress at various levels of government [26]. Despite the availability of these tools, a major gap remains. A 2022 review of the Philippines' digital TB systems by the Stop TB Partnership found that ITIS and its companion applications present all their information as numbers and tables, with no capability to show where cases are clustering on a map at the community level [27].
Given these limitations, there is a clear need for a system that integrates GIS-based spatial analysis into tuberculosis surveillance at the local level. This study proposes BANTAY-TB, a web-based GIS platform designed to support spatial surveillance and hotspot detection of tuberculosis and respiratory diseases in Davao City. The system aims to provide near real-time visualization of case distribution, identify high-risk areas through clustering techniques, and support data-driven decision-making among public health workers.
BANTAY-TB incorporates several features intended to address existing gaps in TB surveillance. These include a GIS-based heatmap with automated hotspot detection, an active case finding module for barangay-level reporting, and a health education component that delivers information on TB and other respiratory diseases in accessible language. The system also includes a multilingual chatbot, a medication adherence monitoring feature with SMS notifications, and a public-facing DOTS center locator that identifies nearby facilities using device GPS. By integrating spatial analysis with existing surveillance practices, BANTAY-TB aims to enhance the accuracy, responsiveness, and effectiveness of tuberculosis control efforts in Davao City.

OBJECTIVES OF THE STUDY
General Objective
The primary goal of this capstone project is to design, develop, and evaluate BANTAY-TB, a comprehensive web-based Geographic Information System (GIS) designed to enhance the surveillance, monitoring, and public accessibility of tuberculosis (TB) data in Davao City.
Specific Objectives
1.	Develop BANTAY-TB with the following features:
◦	A GIS-based spatial surveillance module that visualizes TB cases at the barangay level using heatmaps and DBSCAN clustering for hotspot detection.
◦	A health promotion and education module that provides multilingual (English, Filipino, and Bisaya) information on TB and other respiratory diseases.
◦	A medication adherence monitoring module with SMS notification support for treatment compliance.
◦	A GPS-based DOTS Center Locator for identifying nearby TB treatment facilities.
◦	An active case finding module for logging and tracking presumptive TB cases.
◦	A multilingual AI chatbot for health inquiries and decision support.
2.	Analyze key public health indicators supported by the system, including:
◦	Frequency of TB cases (Active Case Finding);
◦	Health promotion factors (sleep, nutrition, and exercise); and
◦	Patient adherence to medication.
3.	Evaluate the system in terms of functional suitability, usability, and security by conducting Black Box Testing on all modules, and assess user acceptance using the System Usability Scale (SUS).
 
METHODS AND MATERIALS
A. Research Method
This study employs a quantitative research design to guide the development and evaluation of BANTAY-TB, a web-based GIS spatial surveillance and hotspot detection system for tuberculosis and respiratory diseases in Davao City. The quantitative approach utilizes spatial clustering analysis of tuberculosis case data obtained from the Davao City Health Office to validate heatmap outputs and DBSCAN-based hotspot detection against known high-burden barangays.
A descriptive research design is also applied to characterize the spatial distribution patterns of tuberculosis and respiratory disease cases in Davao City and to evaluate the functional performance of all BANTAY-TB modules. Descriptive statistical measures, including frequency counts, percentages, weighted means, and standard deviations, are used to analyze survey responses and system performance metrics.
Data Collection
Data collection for BANTAY-TB is conducted through two quantitative methods to gather the spatial TB case data needed to validate GIS outputs and measure system performance.
TB Case Records from the City Health Office. Anonymized and aggregated TB case data are sourced from the Davao City Health Office in the form of monthly Excel reports (.xlsx format), which the CHO already produces as part of its standard reporting cycle. These records are imported into BANTAY-TB through the bulk Excel data import module, which automatically maps CHO field names to BANTAY-TB system fields, eliminating the need for manual case-by-case encoding. Case records include barangay-level address fields, age, sex, TB classification, and treatment outcome status.
To ensure patient privacy, a de-identification workflow is applied locally on the client side immediately upon file upload, before any data is transmitted to the database. During this process, all personally identifiable information (PII)—including patient full names, specific house numbers, street addresses, and any other direct identifiers present in the uploaded Excel file—is automatically detected and stripped from the dataset within the browser.
The dataset includes the following variables:
•	Barangay-level location
•	Age or age group
•	Sex
•	TB classification (e.g., drug-sensitive, drug-resistant, pulmonary, extrapulmonary)
•	Treatment outcome status
System Usability Scale (SUS) Survey. The SUS instrument is administered to health workers, TB coordinators, and community members who participate in system testing across all three development iterations. A mean SUS score of 68 or above is considered acceptable usability, a score between 68 and 80.3 is considered good, and a score above 80.3 is considered excellent. Final SUS scores are disaggregated by user role and module to identify specific areas requiring refinement before deployment.

Conceptual Framework
[Figure 1: Conceptual Framework of BANTAY-TB — original diagram to be inserted here]
Figure 1. Conceptual Framework of BANTAY-TB
BANTAY-TB is a GIS-based spatial surveillance and hotspot detection system designed for tuberculosis and respiratory diseases in Davao City. It is developed as an independent web-based platform using React.js and Supabase. The system provides functionalities for spatial surveillance, geographic visualization, multilingual AI assistance, health promotion, medication adherence monitoring, and GPS-based facility location to support both disease monitoring and community access to health services.
The system involves five primary user groups: Barangay Admins and Frontliners; Barangay Health Workers (BHWs); Nurses and Doctors; TB Coordinators and Officials; and Patients and Clients. These users interact within a shared web-based environment, each with specific roles and system access.
Barangay Admins and Frontliners serve as the primary operators at the community level. They encode new tuberculosis and respiratory disease cases through the active case finding module, monitor case trends within their barangays, and receive automated DBSCAN hotspot alerts when case density exceeds predefined thresholds.
Barangay Health Workers, Nurses, and Doctors utilize the system for clinical decision support. They monitor patient medication adherence through the adherence tracking module, receive SMS-based alerts for non-adherent patients, analyze GIS heatmap outputs to identify high-risk areas, and use system-generated insights to guide outreach and intervention strategies.
TB Coordinators and Health Officials interact with the system through the administrative layer. They upload monthly case reports from the City Health Office using the bulk Excel import module, configure DBSCAN hotspot detection parameters, manage the DOTS Center Locator database, and review city-wide surveillance data across barangays and districts.
Patients and community members interact with BANTAY-TB through both public and registered access. Without requiring an account, users can access the DOTS Center Locator to identify the nearest treatment facilities using device GPS. Registered patients can access health education content in Tagalog or Bisaya, interact with the multilingual AI chatbot, and report medication adherence through the patient module.
The BANTAY-TB web application functions as the central processing component of the system. It retrieves case data from Supabase and processes it through three main pipelines. The first pipeline handles geospatial visualization, where geocoded case data are plotted using the GIS mapping module through Leaflet.js with GeoJSON barangay boundary overlays. The second pipeline performs spatial clustering analysis using DBSCAN to detect statistically significant disease hotspots. The third pipeline processes patient adherence data, generating SMS notifications for missed medication schedules.
The outputs from these processes are integrated into the system's interactive maps and dashboards, allowing users to filter data by district, date range, and demographic characteristics.
The database serves as the central storage and synchronization layer of the system. It stores user accounts, case records with geographic attributes, chatbot interactions, clustering outputs, adherence records, health education content, and system logs. Supabase Authentication enforces role-based access control, ensuring that sensitive information is accessible only to authorized users.
B. Design Procedure (SDLC Iterative Model)
[Figure 2: Iterative Model — original diagram to be inserted here]
Figure 2. Iterative Model
BANTAY-TB follows an Iterative Software Development Life Cycle (SDLC) model consisting of three structured development cycles. Each iteration builds upon the previous one, ensuring that completed features remain stable while new functionalities are progressively added.
Each iteration follows the same sequence of phases: Initial Planning, Requirements Definition, Analysis and Design, Implementation, Testing, and Evaluation.
Initial Planning
Prior to the first development iteration, the research team establishes the foundational direction, scope, and technical foundation of BANTAY-TB. This phase involves a review of existing tuberculosis digital systems—ITIS, SITB, VITIMES, Race TB, and Care TB—to document their functional limitations and identify the specific capability gaps that BANTAY-TB must address. The team establishes the overall system architecture, selecting React.js as the frontend framework and Supabase as the backend platform, and defines the role-based access structure distinguishing Barangay Admins, BHWs, Nurses, Doctors, TB Coordinators, and community members. The technology stack is confirmed during this phase, including Leaflet.js for GIS map rendering, GeoJSON boundary overlays for barangay-level visualization, DBSCAN via Supabase Functions for hotspot detection, and the OpenAI GPT API for the multilingual chatbot. The team also establishes the three-iteration development roadmap, assigning specific modules to each cycle.
First Iteration
Planning. The first iteration begins with the development team conducting face-to-face consultations with district health officers and TB coordinators from the Davao City Health Office. These consultations identify the specific geographic granularity needed for map-based surveillance decisions and the most critical information TB workers need to see on a case map. The first iteration focuses on delivering the three most operationally critical features: the barangay-level GIS heatmap with automated DBSCAN hotspot detection, the active case finding module, and the public-facing GPS-based DOTS Center Locator.
Requirements. Based on the consultations conducted during the planning phase, the requirements for Iteration 1 are established. The map must display TB case information at the individual barangay level because that is the operational unit at which surveillance decisions and community outreach are initiated. The DBSCAN hotspot detection algorithm must run automatically in the background whenever new case records are added, and must generate near real-time alerts to TB Coordinator accounts when barangay case density exceeds a configured threshold. The active case finding module must allow barangay frontliners to register new cases by disease type, frequency, barangay, age, and sex. The GPS-based DOTS Center Locator must function without requiring any user account or login.
Analysis and Design. High-fidelity UI and UX prototypes are designed for all Iteration 1 modules using Figma. Prototypes include the interactive GIS map page, barangay-level case visualization, DBSCAN hotspot detection layers, filtering options, the active case finding registration form, and the public DOTS Center Locator landing page. The design follows a consistent visual identity for BANTAY-TB, reflecting the system's public health purpose through a clean, accessible interface that works reliably across modern web browsers. Prototypes are reviewed with health workers from selected TB-DOTS facilities in Davao City before the development phase begins.
Implementation. The GIS heatmap screen is built using GeoJSON boundary overlays for all Davao City barangays, rendered through Leaflet.js and populated with TB case data retrieved in real time from Supabase. The DBSCAN hotspot detection algorithm is deployed as a Supabase Function that executes automatically whenever new case records are added to the database. When a barangay exceeds the configured case density threshold, an automated hotspot alert is delivered to the TB Coordinator's notification feed without requiring any manual trigger. The active case finding module is built to allow barangay frontliners to submit new case records by disease type, frequency, and demographic profile. The DOTS Center Locator is implemented as a public-facing screen on the BANTAY-TB landing page, retrieving verified facility records from Supabase and using device GPS to calculate and display the three nearest DOTS centers.
Testing. Iteration 1 modules are evaluated using Black Box Testing. Test cases verify that the GIS heatmap correctly renders barangay-level case density using live Supabase data; that DBSCAN hotspot detection executes automatically upon new case record insertion and that alerts are delivered to the correct TB Coordinator account; that the active case finding module correctly saves new case records with all required fields; and that the DOTS Center Locator correctly identifies and ranks the three nearest facilities based on device GPS coordinates without requiring user login. Each test case specifies a defined input action and a corresponding expected output, with actual system behavior recorded for direct comparison.
Evaluation. At the conclusion of Iteration 1, structured evaluation sessions are conducted with TB-DOTS workers and district health officers to assess the functional performance and usability of the completed modules. Evaluation metrics include: GIS heatmap rendering accuracy, measured by comparing barangay boundary overlays against official Davao City GeoJSON boundary data; correct execution of DBSCAN hotspot detection based on defined parameters, measured as the percentage of test case insertions that correctly generate hotspot alerts; DOTS Center Locator proximity accuracy, measured by comparing GPS-calculated distances against verified facility coordinates; and interface usability, assessed through a SUS survey administered to health worker participants. Feedback collected during evaluation is incorporated before proceeding to Iteration 2.
Second Iteration
Planning. The second iteration begins with a planning review session incorporating the evaluation findings and feedback from Iteration 1. The team confirms the technical requirements for the health promotion and education module, the multilingual AI chatbot, the medication adherence monitoring module with SMS, and the bulk Excel data import module. The planning phase also establishes the column mapping logic required to reconcile CHO monthly Excel report field names with BANTAY-TB system fields, and defines the client-side PII stripping workflow for the bulk import module.
Requirements. The health promotion and education module must deliver content on tuberculosis, pneumonia, COVID-19, and asthma in Tagalog and Bisaya in plain language accessible to community members without medical backgrounds. The multilingual AI chatbot must automatically detect and respond to user queries in English, Filipino, and Bisaya, with a target language detection accuracy of at least 90 percent, subject to system evaluation for queries of three or more words. The medication adherence monitoring module must send SMS notifications to patients on scheduled dose days and generate flagging reports for non-adherent patients. The bulk Excel data import module must accept .xlsx, .xls, and .csv file formats; automatically map CHO field names to BANTAY-TB system fields; display a data preview screen showing the first five imported records for coordinator verification; execute client-side PII stripping prior to any data transmission to Supabase; and trigger immediate re-execution of DBSCAN hotspot detection and GIS heatmap refresh upon successful upload.
Analysis and Design. Prototypes for all Iteration 2 modules are designed using Figma, including the health promotion content pages in Tagalog and Bisaya, the multilingual chatbot interface with language detection preprocessing and conversation history sidebar, the medication adherence dashboard with SMS configuration, and the bulk Excel import screen with automatic column mapping interface, data preview table, and import confirmation screen. All designs are reviewed with health workers prior to implementation.
Implementation. The health promotion and education module is built with condition-specific content pages for TB, pneumonia, COVID-19, and asthma in Tagalog and Bisaya, including guidance on sleep, nutrition, and exercise for each condition. The multilingual AI chatbot is integrated using the OpenAI GPT API with a preprocessing layer for automatic language detection prior to query submission, supporting seamless language switching within a single conversation session and storing session history per authenticated user account in Supabase. The medication adherence monitoring module is implemented with automated SMS notification integration, a patient adherence schedule dashboard, and a non-adherence flagging system for health worker review. The bulk Excel data import module is implemented with automatic column mapping logic, a five-record data preview screen, client-side PII stripping executed in the browser before any upload to Supabase, and an automatic DBSCAN re-execution trigger.
Testing. Test cases for Iteration 2 include: verifying that health promotion content is correctly displayed in Tagalog and Bisaya for each disease condition; submitting chatbot queries in English, Filipino, and Bisaya and verifying correct language detection and appropriate responses; confirming that SMS notifications are delivered to the correct patient accounts on scheduled dose days; uploading a valid CHO .xlsx file and confirming correct row count, automatic column mapping, and data preview accuracy; uploading an unsupported file format and verifying that the system rejects it with an appropriate error message; confirming that DBSCAN hotspot detection re-executes and GIS heatmaps update immediately upon successful import; and verifying that PII fields are stripped client-side and are absent from all Supabase database records. Regression tests also confirm that all Iteration 1 features remain fully functional.
Evaluation. Evaluation of Iteration 2 is conducted with health workers and community members through structured post-testing sessions. Metrics include: chatbot language detection accuracy, measured as the percentage of correctly identified language queries across a defined test set of English, Filipino, and Bisaya inputs of three or more words, with a target accuracy of at least 90 percent; SMS notification delivery rate; bulk import success rate; column auto-mapping accuracy; and data privacy compliance, verified by confirming the complete absence of PII fields in all Supabase records following import. Feedback from these evaluations is incorporated before proceeding to Iteration 3.
Third Iteration
Planning. The third iteration begins with a consolidated review of all outstanding issues, feedback items, and refinement tasks identified during the Iteration 1 and 2 evaluations. The team prioritizes the resolution of any language detection accuracy gaps for Bisaya queries, implements a GPS fallback mechanism for the DOTS Center Locator for scenarios where device GPS is unavailable, and prepares the system for end-to-end testing across all modules simultaneously. The team also finalizes the deployment infrastructure and prepares the full Likert scale usability evaluation survey instrument.
Requirements. Iteration 3 requires that Bisaya language detection accuracy meets or approaches the 90 percent target established in the system objectives, subject to evaluation results, and that all six BANTAY-TB modules function correctly and concurrently under end-to-end testing conditions. All role-based access controls, authentication mechanisms, and Data Privacy Act compliance controls must also pass full regression testing before deployment.
Analysis and Design. Design refinements informed by Iteration 2 evaluation feedback are finalized and applied across all BANTAY-TB interfaces. The deployment architecture is reviewed to confirm that Supabase security rules, authentication configurations, and Supabase Function triggers are correctly configured for production use.
Implementation. Remaining refinements are implemented, including the Bisaya language detection accuracy fix and the GPS locator offline fallback. Any interface or functional improvements identified in prior evaluation sessions are applied. The full system build is prepared for deployment.
Testing. End-to-end Black Box Testing is conducted across all six BANTAY-TB modules simultaneously, covering the complete range of user roles: Barangay Admin, BHW, Nurse, Doctor, TB Coordinator, Patient, and community member. Regression testing verifies that user authentication, role-based access control, and data privacy compliance remain fully functional across the complete integrated system. The full test case suite from Iterations 1 and 2 is re-executed in its entirety to confirm that no previously passed functionality has been disrupted.
Evaluation. Final system evaluation is conducted using the System Usability Scale (SUS) administered to health workers, TB coordinators, BHWs, nurses, doctors, and community members who participated in system testing. The SUS is a standardized 10-item questionnaire designed to assess overall system usability, including effectiveness, efficiency, and user satisfaction.
Respondents provide ratings for each statement based on their level of agreement. The responses are then scored using the standard SUS computation method and converted to a scale of 0 to 100. A benchmark score of 68 or higher is considered acceptable usability, while higher scores indicate better system performance and user satisfaction.
The resulting SUS score represents the overall usability of BANTAY-TB and serves as the primary basis for evaluating system acceptance and readiness for deployment.
C. Testing Procedure
[Figure 3: Black Box Testing Model — original diagram to be inserted here]
Figure 3. Black Box Testing Model
All BANTAY-TB modules are evaluated using the Black Box Testing methodology, in which testers assess system functionality based on inputs and expected outputs without access to the internal code structure or logic. This approach reflects standard validation practices for health information systems, ensuring that system outputs align with defined user requirements across different roles and use cases.
Testers are provided with role-specific test case scripts covering interactions for Barangay Admins, Barangay Health Workers (BHWs), Nurses, Doctors, TB Coordinators, Patients, and community users. Each test case specifies an input action and its corresponding expected output, while actual system behavior is recorded for comparison.
The overall Black Box Testing pass rate is calculated per module as the percentage of test cases that produce the expected results. An intended pass rate of up to 100 percent for defined test cases is targeted, subject to validation during testing, before proceeding to deployment in each development iteration.
Test cases cover all six core modules of BANTAY-TB: the GIS heatmap and hotspot detection system, the active case finding module, the health promotion and education module, the multilingual AI chatbot, the medication adherence monitoring module with SMS notifications, and the GPS-based DOTS Center Locator. Regression testing is also conducted to ensure that supporting features such as user authentication, role-based access control, and data privacy mechanisms remain functional throughout all iterations.
For the bulk Excel data import module, specific test cases include uploading a valid CHO .xlsx file and verifying correct row count and column mapping; uploading unsupported file formats and confirming appropriate error handling; verifying automatic re-execution of DBSCAN hotspot detection and GIS updates after a successful import; and ensuring that all imported records are anonymized in compliance with the Data Privacy Act of 2012 (Republic Act No. 10173) prior to any processing.

This study adheres to established ethical standards for research involving human participants and health-related data. Prior to data collection and system evaluation, ethical clearance will be secured from the University of the Immaculate Conception (UIC). Approval will also be obtained from the College of Computer Studies and the Davao City Health Office to ensure proper authorization for system testing and the use of tuberculosis-related datasets.
Participants for the usability evaluation will be selected using purposive sampling. This approach ensures that individuals with relevant experience and roles in tuberculosis programs and community health services are included in the study.
Participant Selection and Inclusion Criteria
The study will include participants who meet the following criteria:
•	Barangay Health Workers (BHWs), nurses, physicians, TB coordinators, and barangay frontliners currently involved in tuberculosis or community health programs in Davao City;
•	Community members who are potential end-users of the BANTAY-TB system;
•	Individuals aged 18 years and above;
•	Individuals who have basic familiarity with mobile phones or web-based applications; and
•	Individuals who are willing to participate and provide informed consent.
Exclusion Criteria
The following individuals will be excluded from participation:
•	Individuals below 18 years of age;
•	Individuals not residing or working in Davao City;
•	Individuals without any involvement in health-related programs or no potential to use the system; and
•	Individuals who decline or withdraw informed consent.
All participants will be required to provide informed consent prior to participation. The purpose of the study, procedures involved, duration of participation, and the voluntary nature of participation will be clearly explained. Participants will be informed that they may withdraw at any time without penalty. Confidentiality and anonymity of responses will be strictly maintained.
This study strictly complies with the Data Privacy Act of 2012 (Republic Act No. 10173). To ensure data privacy, a client-side de-identification procedure is implemented within the BANTAY-TB system prior to any data transmission or storage. This process automatically removes all personally identifiable information (PII)—including patient names, house numbers, and street-level addresses—within the user's browser environment. Only non-identifiable and analysis-relevant data are retained, including barangay location, age group, sex, TB classification, and treatment outcome status. These data are used solely for spatial analysis, hotspot detection, and system evaluation. No information that can directly or indirectly identify individuals is stored in the database.
Role-based access control is implemented to ensure that only authorized users, such as health workers and TB coordinators, can access restricted system features. Public users are limited to non-sensitive functionalities such as the DOTS Center Locator and health education modules.
All collected data will be used strictly for academic and research purposes and will not be disclosed to unauthorized individuals or third parties. The researchers commit to upholding confidentiality, data minimization, and responsible data handling throughout the duration of the study.
